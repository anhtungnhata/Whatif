## 0. LOGLINE

When the What-If Engine drops Professor Brick, Max and Luna onto the frozen plains of Mars, they discover that every single breath, sip of water and potato has to be engineered — and that the Red Planet is quietly trying to kill them. Built on real NASA data, the episode answers the question honestly: yes, LEGO humans could live on Mars… but only underground, inside bubbles, making everything themselves.

### 2.5 Camera & 16:9 framing rules

1. Every frame is composed for **16:9 (1.78:1)**. No letterboxing, no vertical crops. Keep action inside the 90% action-safe area; keep on-screen text inside the 80% title-safe area, lower third.
2. **180° rule / screen positions:** in group shots, Professor Brick is frame-LEFT, Max CENTER, Luna frame-RIGHT. Forward progress (journey, walking toward goals) moves LEFT → RIGHT.
3. Eye-lines: minifig head height sits on the upper-third line in mediums.
4. Lenses: macro 100 mm for close-ups, 50 mm for mediums, 24–35 mm for wides, 14 mm for epic establishing shots. Shallow tilt-shift depth of field to sell the toy scale.
5. Minifig motion: authentic LEGO articulation only — arms swing at the shoulder, hands rotate, legs move as a pair or alternate, heads turn; no bending elbows or knees.

### 2.6 Global style tokens & negative prompt

- **Style (included in every prompt):** photorealistic 3D LEGO animation, glossy ABS plastic with visible studs, subtle scratches and mold seams, macro tilt-shift depth of field, cinematic color grading, physically based rendering, 16:9 widescreen.
- **Global negative prompt (append to every generation):** `human skin, realistic humans, bendable elbows, extra fingers, five-fingered hands, melted plastic, warped bricks, blurry faces, inconsistent hair color, missing name patch, vertical frame, letterbox bars, text artifacts, watermark, logo, extra characters`

- Spoken text per shot averages ≈ 22 words and never exceeds 28 (max ≈ 2.8 words/second); the densest shots (09, 14, 28, 31) should be read briskly with no pre-roll.

### 5.4 Sound-design rule for Mars exteriors
Mars' thin air carries sound poorly and quieter (and higher-pitched sounds fade fastest). For exteriors, keep wind and impacts **muffled and thin**, and let suit-internal sounds (breathing, radio, alarms) dominate. This sells realism and contrasts with the cozy, full sound inside the habitat.
